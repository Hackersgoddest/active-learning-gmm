# Before RQ1: separation, the error floor, and what entropy can and can't show

Read this **after** your EM code works and **before** the first RQ1 experiment.
It uses the separation $c$ from Lesson 1, Section 1.7, and the responsibilities from
Section 1.4. This version is written slower and smaller than the first pass, with a
worked number after every formula; if a formula still doesn't land, stop
right there and tell me which one.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## P.0  Why this note exists: the problem in plain words

RQ1 asks: *if I push two clusters closer together, does EM's job get harder,
and in exactly what way?* "Harder" is too vague to test. This note turns it
into three precise, checkable things:

1. **A ruler for "how close is too close."** One number, $c$, that says how
   separated two clusters are, so "closer together" has an exact meaning.
2. **A speed limit that exists even for a perfect model.** Before we ask how
   well *EM* recovers the clusters, we need to know how well *anything* could,
   even a method that already knows the true answer. That's the Bayes
   error.
3. **An exact formula for "how confused is the model here."** So that when
   RQ1 talks about "points near the boundary between clusters," that phrase
   means one specific, fixed thing, not something that shifts depending on
   what the model happens to output.

Setting for this whole note: $K=2$ components, equal weight
($\pi_1=\pi_2=\tfrac12$), the same spread for both
($\boldsymbol\Sigma = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}$, a circle, not an ellipse). This is
the simplest possible two-cluster case, on purpose, so every number below can
be checked by hand.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## P.1  The separation number $c$

**Picture it first.** Two bell curves with the same width $\sigma$, centred at
$\boldsymbol\mu_1$ and $\boldsymbol\mu_2$. $c$ just answers: *how many
"widths" apart are the two centres?*

$$
c=\frac{\lVert\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_2\rVert}{\sigma}
$$

**Worked example, 1-D first (easiest case to see).** Say $\sigma=2$,
$\mu_1=0$, $\mu_2=6$. The centres are $6$ apart, and each "unit" is $2$ wide,
so

$$
c = 6/2 = 3.
$$

Reading: *the two centres sit 3 standard deviations apart.* That's it: the
2-D formula above is the exact same idea, just using the ordinary 2-D distance
$\lVert\cdot\rVert$ instead of 1-D subtraction.

**Rule of thumb, to build intuition before the numbers in P.2 below:**
$c \ge 4$: clusters barely touch. $c \approx 2$: clearly overlapping, but
still mostly separable. $c \le 1$: heavily overlapping, close to one blob.

**Optional, skip unless you're reading outside papers:** the literature's
usual version of $c$ (Dasgupta, FOCS 1999) is not quite ours; for our
spherical, equal-$\sigma$ setup, converting is $c_{\text{ours}} = \sqrt{D}\,c_{\text{Dasgupta}}$.
In 2-D that's a factor of $\sqrt2\approx1.41$. Not needed for RQ1 itself.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## P.2  Even a perfect model makes mistakes: the Bayes error floor

**The thought experiment.** Imagine an oracle that already knows the *true*
$\boldsymbol\mu_1$, $\boldsymbol\mu_2$, $\sigma$ exactly, no estimating
anything. Given a new point, it does the obviously-best thing: assign it to
whichever mean is closer. Call this the **Bayes-optimal classifier**: nothing
can do better, by definition, because it isn't guessing at anything.

**Even this oracle is sometimes wrong.** Why? The two bell curves overlap.
Some points truly generated by cluster 1 will, just by bad luck, land closer
to $\boldsymbol\mu_2$ than to $\boldsymbol\mu_1$ (a rare draw from cluster 1's
tail, reaching across the midpoint). The oracle correctly judges "closer to
$\mu_2$," and is correctly fooled, because the data itself was ambiguous. This
isn't a flaw in the classifier; it's the actual, irreducible cost of the
clusters overlapping at all.

**The formula:**

$$
\text{Bayes error} = \Phi\!\left(-\tfrac{c}{2}\right)
$$

$\Phi$ (the standard normal CDF, `scipy.stats.norm.cdf`) is a lookup: *"what
fraction of a standard bell curve lies below this value?"* It's the same
table/function you'd use for any basic statistics z-score question.

**Worked example.** Take $\sigma=1$, $\mu_1=-1$, $\mu_2=1$, so $c=2$:

$$
\text{Bayes error} = \Phi(-1) = 0.1587.
$$

Reading: **even with perfect knowledge of the true parameters, about 15.9% of
points get misclassified, purely because the clusters overlap that much at
$c=2$.** This has nothing to do with EM yet; EM hasn't been run.

More values, for intuition (all computed the same way):

| $c$ | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bayes error | 0.401 | 0.309 | 0.159 | 0.067 | 0.023 | 0.0013 |

**Why RQ1 needs this.** Your hypothesis says accuracy drops as $c$ shrinks,
true, but partly for a boring reason (geometry), not an interesting one (EM
struggling). The number worth reporting in RQ1 isn't EM's raw accuracy, it's
the **gap**: `EM's accuracy` $-$ `Bayes-optimal accuracy` $(1-\Phi(-c/2))$. A
gap near zero means EM is doing as well as anything possibly could at that
separation. A gap that grows as $c$ shrinks means EM itself is adding extra
error on top of the unavoidable overlap, and *that's* the real finding.

## P.3  What a responsibility looks like here, exactly

**The idea first.** In this simple two-cluster setup, a point's responsibility
doesn't actually depend on its full 2-D position. It depends on exactly **one
number**: how far the point sits along the line connecting
$\boldsymbol\mu_1$ and $\boldsymbol\mu_2$. Moving a point *sideways*,
perpendicular to that line, doesn't change its responsibility at all; only
moving it *along* that line does.

Call that one number $u$ (a signed distance along the line, measured in a
particular scaled way):

$$
u_n = \frac{(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_2)^\top\!\big(\mathbf{x}_n-\tfrac12(\boldsymbol\mu_1+\boldsymbol\mu_2)\big)}{\sigma^2}
$$

Reading: take the point, subtract off the midpoint (so the midpoint becomes
"zero"), and measure how far that is in the direction from $\mu_2$ toward
$\mu_1$. $u_n$ is positive on $\mu_1$'s side, negative on $\mu_2$'s side, and
exactly $0$ right at the midpoint.

**Turning $u$ into a responsibility: the sigmoid function.** A quick refresher
if logistic functions feel unfamiliar:

$$
\operatorname{sigmoid}(u) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}
$$

It's an S-shaped curve: $\operatorname{sigmoid}(0) = 0.5$ exactly; as
$u \to +\infty$ it approaches $1$; as $u\to-\infty$ it approaches $0$. It takes
any real number and smoothly turns it into something between 0 and 1, exactly
what a probability needs to be.

$$
r_{n1} = \operatorname{sigmoid}(u_n)
$$

(Checked numerically against the direct Bayes'-rule formula from Section 1.4:
agreement to $10^{-16}$: this isn't an approximation, it's the same formula,
simplified using this problem's symmetry.)

**Worked example.** Take $\boldsymbol\mu_1=(3,0)$, $\boldsymbol\mu_2=(-3,0)$,
$\sigma=1$ (so $c = 6$, well separated). A few points:

| point $\mathbf{x}$ | $u$ | $r_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $(0,0)$, the midpoint | $0$ | $0.500$ |
| $(1,0)$, one step toward $\mu_1$ | $6$ | $0.997$ |
| $(3,0)$, right at $\mu_1$ | $18$ | $\approx 1.000$ |
| $(-3,0)$, right at $\mu_2$ | $-18$ | $\approx 0.000$ |

Reading: step just one unit off the midpoint, toward $\mu_1$, and the model is
already 99.7% sure. That's what "well separated" looks like in responsibility
terms.

**The fact that matters most for RQ1: the midpoint is always a coin flip.**
Whatever $c$ is, huge or tiny, the exact midpoint always has $u=0$, so
$\mathbf{r} = (0.5, 0.5)$ and entropy $\mathbb{H}=\ln 2$, every time. Separation
doesn't change the midpoint's own entropy at all.

**What separation actually changes: how fast entropy falls off *away* from the
midpoint.** Same offset, one unit from the midpoint, two different
separations:

| $c$ | responsibilities at offset $1$ | entropy $\mathbb{H}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ (heavy overlap) | $(0.731,\ 0.269)$ | $0.582$ |
| $6$ (well separated) | $(0.997,\ 0.003)$ | $0.017$ |

Reading: at $c=1$, a point just one unit from the midpoint is still quite
uncertain (close to the $\ln 2 = 0.693$ maximum). At $c=6$, that same one-unit
step already makes the model almost completely certain. **This** is where
separation shows up: not at the midpoint itself, but in how quickly certainty
builds as you move away from it.

**Why this matters for RQ1's design.** "Points near the midpoint" has to mean
a fixed *geometric* region, e.g. "within $0.5\sigma$ of the true midpoint",
decided before looking at any responsibilities. Defining it instead as "points
whose responsibilities happen to be close to $0.5$" would be circular: at
small $c$ that region is huge, at large $c$ it's tiny, so you'd be comparing
different-sized regions across conditions and calling it the same metric.
